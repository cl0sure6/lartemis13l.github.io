*Six preprints, October 2026. All six were produced with substantial AI assistance under my direction — the full statement is at the [bottom of this page](#status-and-attribution).*

## The program

A first-time singularity of the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations would have to concentrate vorticity. These papers study one kind of concentration in detail — a slender vortex tube in an external strain — through the exact two-dimensional reduction of a tube in a uniform strain, the asymmetric Burgers problem. Through the Biot–Savart law the velocity a tube feels splits into the field of the other vortices and its own: the first tears it, the second holds it together.

The first three papers make the local mechanisms precise: self-induction protects the tube, a torn tube leaks circulation at an exponentially slow rate, and it drifts off its stagnation point. The last three ask what those mechanisms say about an actual singularity: what a tube approaching a singular point would need, how every way of concentrating vorticity ends either in one of these exclusions or in a Liouville-type problem, and what single quantity a Liouville theorem for Type I profiles would have to control.

**Where it ends.** Nothing here proves regularity. The exclusions hold under stated hypotheses — a coherence hypothesis for the leak, weighted-energy and weak-L³ controls that known necessary conditions suggest fail at actual singularities — and the covering ends in three Liouville problems, a Navier–Stokes one for self-stretching clusters, an Euler one and a discretely self-similar one, which the papers name and do not solve.

## The six papers

**Self-Induction and the Protective Tilt: A Heat-Kernel Monotonicity for Vortices in Asymmetric Strain** — 8 pp. Constructs a circulation weighted by a backward heat kernel adapted to the strain, for which the strain and the viscosity cancel exactly, so that its rate of change is the self-induced term alone. That term has the sign of a single geometric quantity, the tilt of the vortex relative to the strain axes, and by exact moment identities it is non-negative precisely when the vortex is rounder than the passive field that would occupy its place. The protective sign is proved for weak vortices at every asymmetry λ < 1; to first order in λ at every circulation, by an energy argument built on the skew-symmetry of the linearised Biot–Savart operator found by Gallay and Wayne; and for large circulation at every λ < 1. The time-dependent version is proved at first order in λ for early and late times, and at intermediate circulation every sign holds numerically. In the torn regime λ > 1, where no steady vortex exists, the lifetime gained over the passive field collapses onto a single curve of the scale-invariant ratio ε = 2πλν/Γ, with a threshold near ε ≈ 0.05. [Preprint](/preprints/self-induction-protective-tilt.pdf)

**Kramers Escape from Strained Vortices: The Leak Law, Its Certified Exponent, and the Survival Threshold** — 9 pp. In the tearing regime λ > 1 a viscous tube has no steady state and loses circulation by an exponentially slow leak: its vorticity is the density of stochastic Lagrangian particles, and the leak is their escape over the saddle of the strain-plus-vortex drift. For a frozen drift the exponent is a Freidlin–Wentzell quasipotential, κ ∼ e^{−c(λ)/ε}. The paper computes c(λ) by minimum action (c(2) = 0.2287, c(3) = 0.2098), certifies upper bounds (c(2) ≤ 0.2331, c(3) ≤ 0.2185), and derives the large-λ limit c∞ = 0.19258; the form of the law is due to Moffatt, Kida and Ohkitani and to Prochazka and Pullin, whose constant 1/(2e) lies 4.7% below that limit. Direct simulations at λ = 2 over four decades of κ give a pure exponential. For the true, time-dependent drift it proves a one-sided, quantitative version — a lower bound on the loss per window, by Girsanov — under a coherence hypothesis, identifies the survival threshold as the leak's runaway, and gives numerical evidence for a metastable spectral gap. [Preprint](/preprints/kramers-escape-strained-vortices.pdf)

**Drift, Tracking and the Stability of Vortex Tubes in Tearing Strain** — 6 pp. In the exact reduction the first moment of vorticity obeys an exact linear equation for any sign structure, so in the tearing regime a tube drifts off its stagnation point at the rate (λ − 1)/2, whatever its structure. Can surrounding vortices hold it? For relative equilibria of same-sign point vortices the paper proves they cannot: after a √Γ rescaling the linearisation is complex symmetric, so the coupled drift rate is at least the rate with the others frozen. Opposite-sign partners break the symmetry and can hold the core in a neutral dipole, which is what ensembles show; for time-dependent same-sign supply the inequality fails pointwise but holds in the median. In three dimensions short axial waves are held by self-induction and stretched into the planar drift, the elliptic and curvature instabilities give bounded gains, and tilting maps λ > 3 into (1, 3). [Preprint](/preprints/drift-tracking-vortex-tubes.pdf)

**Circulation in Leray Variables: Gathering, Local Energy, and an Outgoing Transport Law** — 6 pp. In backward similarity variables the vorticity of a Navier–Stokes solution is frozen into an outward field; together with the leak law, this says what a vortex tube approaching a singular point would need. If its accumulated strain time τ diverges, the cumulative leak is infinite unless its circulation grows like ln τ: a tube that survives must gather circulation. An elementary inequality bounds averaged circulation by the scale-invariant local energy, so gathering requires that energy to grow like (ln τ)²; for a model gathering tube this places the scenario in Seregin's weighted class, where his theorem excludes it whenever his weighted energy bound holds with the natural weight. Under weak-L³ control outside the backward paraboloid — the hypothesis of Barker, Fernández-Dalgo and Prange — circulation is bounded, gathering is impossible, and sphere-averaged circulation obeys an outgoing transport law with no reservoir beyond a finite Leray radius. The paper notes that known necessary conditions suggest this control fails at actual singularities, and says what that leaves. [Preprint](/preprints/circulation-leray-variables.pdf)

**A Covering for Collapse: Tubes, Sheets, Clusters, and the Reduction to Liouville Problems** — 6 pp. Organises the ways a first-time singularity could concentrate vorticity and shows that, modulo stated hypotheses, each ends either in an exclusion by the mechanisms of the companion papers — leak, drift, supply — or in a Liouville-type problem. The key new estimate bounds the strain at a collapsing core due to vorticity beyond distance r by C√A/r², with A the scale-invariant local energy; since compression needs a strain of at least 2ν over the squared core radius, the strain of any collapse comes from within a bounded number of core radii, whatever the number or sign of distant structures. With a dichotomy for weak-L³ mass outside the backward paraboloid, spread concentration goes to Seregin's weighted framework and sparse concentration to near clusters. A torn tube's own sheet does not re-protect it; sheets pooled from several tubes re-form a core only below a pooled threshold, and the re-formed whole is then carried off by the drift. What remains are three Liouville problems — a Navier–Stokes one for self-stretching clusters, an Euler one, and a discretely self-similar one — which the paper states and does not solve. [Preprint](/preprints/covering-for-collapse.pdf)

**A Time-Averaged Bernoulli Identity for Type I Profiles of the Navier–Stokes Equations** — 7 pp. In backward similarity variables the head pressure of a Navier–Stokes profile satisfies a transport–diffusion identity forced by the enstrophy density. Pairing it with a time-dependent weight that solves the adjoint equation — a time-reversed Fokker–Planck equation, whose pullback invariant density the paper constructs with two-sided Gaussian bounds — gives an exact averaged identity for every profile with a global Type I bound and every time window, exact over a period for discretely self-similar profiles. For stationary profiles its right side vanishes (Nečas–Růžička–Šverák, Tsai); for rotated profiles it is Pineau–Vicol's small-rotation term. In general the whole obstruction to a Liouville theorem is a single averaged correlation between the pressure and the motion of the weight: if it is small, the profile is trivial, with no assumption on the period. A localized version gives a necessary condition for any Type I singularity in the sense of Pineau–Vicol. Model computations show the correlation is small for thin-cored vortex clusters but grows with core size and amplitude without visible saturation, so it must be controlled by its sign or dynamics rather than its size. [Preprint](/preprints/time-averaged-bernoulli-identity.pdf)

## Status and attribution

**Proof status.** Each paper separates what it proves from what it measures, and the summaries above follow that line. The self-induction paper proves the protective sign in three regimes and checks the remaining one numerically. The leak paper computes its exponents by minimum action, certifies upper bounds, and proves its one-sided version only under a coherence hypothesis. The Leray-variable and covering papers hold modulo stated hypotheses. The Bernoulli identity is proved, while its correlation term is only computed in models. None of the six resolves the regularity problem, and none claims to.

**Attribution.** Each paper carries an acknowledgement stating that it was compiled with the help of generative AI. The direction is mine — for the self-induction paper, the tearing reading of external strain, the electromagnetic reading of self-induction, and the request for a heat-kernel monotonicity — and the AI carried out the derivations, the computations and the drafting under my supervision. I am responsible for the contents, including any errors. Not yet independently reviewed; corrections welcome.
